Ferrari F12 vs 458 Italia: V12 character, supercar agility and the details that matter

The F12berlinetta combines a spectacular V12 with grand-touring proportions. The 458 Italia brings a 4.5-litre, 9,000-rpm V8 to a lighter mid-engine chassis. Their strengths explain why the 458 is such a compelling driving car and the F12 such an appealing V12 GT.

09.10.2026 · Luxury Obsession

A Ferrari comparison should start with what makes each car special. The 458 Italia has a 4.5-litre naturally aspirated V8 producing 570 CV at 9,000 rpm, a seven-speed dual-clutch gearbox and a mid-engine layout. These are the ingredients of a serious supercar, with lower mass and compact proportions that give it a particularly strong case on winding roads. The F12berlinetta answers with a 6.3-litre V12, 740 CV and the long-bonnet silhouette of a two-seat grand tourer.

The distinction is between two accomplished expressions of Ferrari engineering. The 458 concentrates the experience around a high-revving V8 and an agile chassis. The F12 adds twelve-cylinder theatre and greater touring flexibility. Its hydraulic steering, integrated aerodynamics and carefully considered details also give it a distinctive appeal beside the later 812 Superfast and 12Cilindri. Understanding those details makes this a more useful comparison than simply choosing the highest power figure.

The 458’s 4.5-litre V8 and mid-engine character

The 458’s engine is an attraction in its own right. Its 570 CV from 4.5 litres equates to approximately 127 CV per litre without turbocharging, with peak power arriving at 9,000 rpm. [5] That combination gives the Italia a clear identity: the reward is in response, revs and the relationship between accelerator, gearbox and road, rather than the effortless delivery of a much larger torque figure.

It also puts the F12 comparison into perspective. Four fewer cylinders do not make the 458 a diluted Ferrari. Its engine sits behind the occupants in a body built around the mid-engine concept, and the lower published dry weight reduces the mass the chassis must manage. On an itinerary made up of climbs, linked bends and frequent changes of direction, those are relevant strengths.

For a driver who wants the journey to revolve around cornering rhythm and the sound of a naturally aspirated engine, the 458 offers a complete proposition. The F12’s greater output gives it a different character, rather than automatically a more rewarding one.

A V12 with two distinct sides

The F12’s 6,262 cc, 65-degree naturally aspirated V12 produces 740 CV at 8,250 rpm and 690 Nm at 6,000 rpm, with an 8,700 rpm limit. Those last two numbers matter together. There is substantial torque to work with, but peak power still arrives high in the rev range. This is a large engine with a reason to keep revving, rather than one that has finished its best work halfway up the dial. [1]

The combination gives the F12 two useful characteristics: substantial torque for touring and a power peak that rewards using the upper rev range. Its seven-speed dual-clutch gearbox allows the driver to select gears with the paddles or use automatic operation. The V12 therefore belongs in a grand tourer without losing the high-revving nature associated with Ferrari’s sports cars.

A detail behind the drama: useful torque well before the red line

One less obvious F12 detail is that Ferrari quoted 80 per cent of maximum torque as available from 2,500 rpm. The same engine uses 200-bar direct injection and reaches its power peak at 8,250 rpm. [11] The interest lies in the breadth: its character is not confined to a narrow band near the limiter.

That helps explain why an engine capable of such dramatic high-rev performance can also work in a grand tourer. The driver need not treat every section of a journey as an exercise in chasing the next downshift. When the road and conditions permit, the upper rev range remains a distinct part of the experience. This flexibility is one of the F12’s strongest arguments as a V12 GT.

Front engine does not mean all the weight is at the front

The F12 uses a front-mid-engine layout with a rear transaxle: the gearbox sits at the back, helping distribute mass along the car. Ferrari quotes 46 per cent of the weight over the front axle and 54 per cent over the rear. Its seven-speed dual-clutch transmission drives the rear wheels. Reading it as a conventional nose-heavy coupé therefore misses the point. [2]

The layout also changes the view from the driving seat and the packaging of the cabin and luggage space. In the F12, the long bonnet stretches ahead of the occupants. In the 458, the engine sits behind them and the main luggage compartment is at the front. These are different ways of organising a sports car, rather than a simple division between a capable and an incapable chassis.

Twelve alloys beneath the bodywork

The F12’s aluminium structure is another detail that headline power figures can obscure. Ferrari describes the use of twelve different alloy types in its construction. [12] That reflects an approach in which the material is selected for the task, rather than treating every part of the structure as the same piece of metal.

A body has to satisfy several demands at once: stiffness, weight, load paths, manufacturing and the way its components are joined. The alloy count is interesting because it reveals the engineering beneath an apparently traditional long-bonnet coupé. The F12’s appeal is therefore more than an exceptional engine placed in an elegant shell; the shell is part of the performance project.

The Aero Bridge: the beautiful part is doing a job

The F12’s sculpted front wings are more than decoration. The Aero Bridge directs airflow from the bonnet towards the sides of the car to help generate downforce. Active Brake Cooling adds movable vanes to the brake-cooling ducts, opening them when cooling is needed and reducing drag when closed. [3]

The design was developed by the Ferrari Styling Centre in collaboration with Pininfarina. [4] The air passages are integrated into the bonnet and flanks, making the aerodynamic treatment a defining part of the body shape. The long bonnet and rearward cabin distinguish it clearly from the mid-engine proportions of the 458.

Ferrari’s brochure quotes a drag coefficient of 0.299 and 123 kg of downforce at 200 km/h for the F12. [13] These figures put the Aero Bridge and brake-cooling vanes into context: the body was developed to manage both resistance and aerodynamic load, while retaining recognisable GT proportions.

The brake-cooling arrangement is particularly revealing. Keeping the vanes open all the time would not be the most efficient solution; opening them when cooling is required reconciles two competing demands. The same design logic gives the Aero Bridge its interest: a passage shaped into the body does useful work without becoming an oversized external appendage.

The quoted downforce is a measurement at a specified speed, not a constant load available at any road speed. Its significance here is the development effort behind the shape, rather than a promise about ordinary-road cornering.

The F1-inspired rear fog light and the Kamm-style tail

Look low in the centre of the rear and the F12 has a small detail with a clear motorsport connection: Ferrari describes its rear fog light as F1-inspired. The rear also combines circular tail lamps with a Kamm-style treatment and the vertical elements of the diffuser, creating a distinctive T-shaped composition. [1] It is a design reference to Formula 1, rather than a claim that the road car uses an identical racing component.

This is one of the details that rewards looking beyond the familiar front three-quarter view. The long bonnet communicates the grand-tourer layout; the concentrated lighting and diffuser treatment at the back express a more competition-influenced identity. Together they explain how the F12 can look elegant and purposeful without relying on a large rear wing.

Ferrari F12 vs 458: weight, dimensions and agility

The 458 has 170 CV less than the F12, but Ferrari’s published dry figures put it 145 kg lighter. Its wheelbase is 70 mm shorter and its body 91 mm shorter and 60 mm lower. Width is almost identical: the F12 is only 5 mm wider. The distinction is therefore more about mass, engine position and proportions than a dramatically narrower footprint. [9]

Lower mass means less inertia to manage under braking and during changes of direction. Together with its mid-engine layout, this helps explain the 458’s appeal on roads with linked bends, climbs and changing gradients. Its supercar character centres on placing the car accurately, working with the gearbox and building a rhythm through corners.

The F12 pairs greater mass with substantially more power and torque. Its rear-biased weight distribution and direct steering make it a performance-focused GT, rather than a soft touring coupé. The Straight Pipes review discussed below is particularly useful on this point: its reported steering impressions underline how responsive the F12 can be. Weight and dimensions help explain the distinction, but do not establish a universal cornering-speed or lap-time ranking.

For more detail on the V8 car’s engine and design, see the Ferrari 458 Italia guide.

A two-seat GT, including the space behind the seats

The F12’s touring brief is visible inside as well. Ferrari’s description includes an upholstered rear shelf or bench area with leather straps for securing bags. [1] This is luggage space, not a second row of passenger seats: the F12 remains a two-seater.

That distinction matters when imagining a trip for two. The appeal is not simply carrying more objects, but having an interior organised around travelling with them. It complements the rear luggage area and helps explain why the F12 suits a longer GT itinerary. The 458 makes a different trade-off, prioritising its mid-engine arrangement and asking the travellers to pack more selectively.

F12 or 458 for two people: luggage and touring

The F12’s rear luggage area and GT layout make it the more practical of these two choices for a couple carrying more bags or planning a longer touring itinerary. The 458’s front luggage compartment calls for a more selective packing approach.

For lighter travel, a soft weekender bag and a small backpack per person provide a useful starting point for planning a 458 trip. Fit depends on the dimensions and fullness of the individual bags; this is not a guarantee that any four bags will fit. Share the measurements before booking so the arrangement can be checked and everything safely stowed.

The practical distinction is straightforward: the F12 offers more luggage flexibility, while the 458 suits travellers willing to pack around a more compact, mid-engine sports car.

Ferrari F12 vs 812 Superfast: what the successor improves

The 812 Superfast develops the front-engine V12 formula with 6,496 cc and 800 CV at 8,500 rpm, an increase of 60 CV over the F12. It retains the naturally aspirated engine and rear-wheel drive, while introducing electric power steering to Ferrari’s range and combining it with rear-wheel steering through Virtual Short Wheelbase 2.0. [6]

These changes make the 812 a substantial evolution. It combines greater engine output with a more integrated approach to steering and chassis control, extending the capabilities of the V12 GT rather than changing its fundamental identity. It remains a compelling car in its own right: a high-revving twelve-cylinder engine, two-seat touring layout and sophisticated chassis in one package.

The standard F12berlinetta uses hydraulic power assistance and does not have the rear-wheel-steering system introduced on the F12tdf. [7] [8]

Why the F12 appeals to enthusiasts seeking a more mechanical feel

The F12’s case against the 812 is strongest when the comparison moves from outright capability to the way that capability is experienced. Hydraulic steering and the absence of rear-wheel steering on the standard berlinetta give it an earlier configuration of controls. For an enthusiast who values that arrangement, the F12 offers a reason to seek out the predecessor deliberately, rather than simply accept it as the older car.

A contemporary Automobile first drive praised the F12’s steering communication and described a car whose electronic assistance remained unobtrusive from the driver’s seat. [14] That helps explain its reputation for an immediate, mechanical character: the attraction is the sensation of direct responses, not a literal absence of electronics. Its dual-clutch transmission and electronic chassis systems are integral to the car.

The 812 takes that formula further with more power and additional chassis control. Forza’s road test found its steering related in feel to the F12’s and described the newer systems as subtle aids to exploiting its ability. [15] The useful distinction is therefore the F12’s rawer appeal to drivers who favour the earlier setup, alongside the 812’s broader technical capabilities. Both are enthusiast cars; the F12’s specific strengths remain compelling even when the successor is objectively more advanced.

Where the Ferrari 12Cilindri fits

The 12Cilindri continues the front-mid-engine V12 lineage after the 812 Superfast. Its naturally aspirated 6.5-litre engine produces 830 CV and reaches a 9,500 rpm limit, paired with an eight-speed dual-clutch gearbox. [10]

It also gives the concept a different visual identity, with a more geometric, retro-futuristic treatment than the flowing F12. Across these three generations, the underlying appeal remains a naturally aspirated V12 ahead of a two-seat cabin. The differences lie in their design, controls and degree of technical development, rather than a simple progression from character to its absence.

A few questions about the F12

How do the F12 and 458 differ on winding roads?

The 458 has a lower published dry weight, a shorter wheelbase and a mid-engine layout. The F12 combines more power and torque with rear-biased weight distribution and direct steering. These characteristics shape different driving experiences; they do not prove that either car is faster through every corner.

Is the F12 an alternative to the 458?

Yes, if you are comparing experiences rather than looking for the same layout. The F12 is a front-mid-engine V12 GT; the 458 Italia is a mid-rear-engine V8 supercar.

What changes between the F12 and 812 Superfast?

The 812 adds 60 CV, increases engine capacity to 6.5 litres and combines electric power steering with rear-wheel steering. The standard F12berlinetta uses hydraulic power assistance without rear-wheel steering. Both are naturally aspirated V12 GTs with rear-wheel drive.

Luxury Obsession’s view: 458 for the curves, F12 among the V12s

For a couple travelling light and building a Swiss driving holiday around winding roads, our choice is the Ferrari 458 Italia. Its lower mass, mid-engine layout and racier character are the qualities we value most for that kind of journey. With a weekender bag and a small backpack each, subject to a fit check, it is the Ferrari we would choose to make the drive the centre of the holiday.

For more luggage and a V12 grand tour, we would choose the F12berlinetta. Its proportions and combination of V12 performance with GT practicality make it our favourite of the three twelve-cylinder cars discussed here. The 812 Superfast is also a great Ferrari, with genuine performance and chassis advances; preferring the F12 does not diminish those strengths. The 12Cilindri is technically impressive, but its styling appeals to us less. That final preference is about taste, while the differences in the comparison above explain what each car offers.
